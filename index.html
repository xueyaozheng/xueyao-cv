Individualized Reorganization and Neural Mechanisms of Episodic Memory

Zheng, X., Axmacher, N., & Xue, G.* (under preparation)
Under the guidance of Prof. Xue and Prof. Axmacher, I designed an ecologically valid fMRI study to investigate how semantic relationships drive the reorganization of naturalistic memories. Using large language models, I developed a novel metric system to quantitatively capture memory reconstruction in both content and sequence. Furthermore, we explored the neural mechanisms underlying this process, revealing that the strength of reactivation of semantically related events in the hippocampus and medial prefrontal cortex at event boundaries predicts individual differences in semantic-driven reorganization. This research establishes a direct brain-behavior link, demonstrating that memory reconstruction is guided by semantic knowledge and supported by dynamic neural reactivation.

RESEARCH INTEREST

A Unified Computational Framework for Individual Differences in Narrative Generalization
This direction aims to explain individual variability in how people generalize knowledge when experiencing naturalistic narratives. I propose that this variability originates from an inherent trade-off between pattern separation and integration within the prefrontal-hippocampal-neocortical system. I hypothesize that each individual possesses a latent cognitive style, a reproducible bias along the separation-integration continuum, which governs how they distill discrete experiences into generalized knowledge. The core objective is to map this latent computational bias onto individual differences in neural representational geometry (e.g., the orthogonality or alignment of neural subspaces), and to demonstrate how this geometry systematically predicts generalization behavior. A key question is how top-down control signals, potentially carried by low-frequency oscillations, from prefrontal cortex sculpt these hippocampal-neocortical representational geometries to bias towards separation or integration.

Compositional Inference through Structured Priors: Hippocampal Replay at Event Boundaries Guides Social Behavior
This direction aims to elucidate how the brain enables flexible social inference in naturalistic contexts. We propose that event boundaries trigger the reactivation of structured social knowledge, transforming it into structured priors to guide Bayesian inference. This process likely involves coordinated dynamics between the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex, possibly mediated by specific oscillatory coupling (e.g., theta-gamma coupling) that facilitates the communication of prior information. The transformation of reactivated patterns into priors may be instantiated through a spatial computing mechanism, where contextual priors are applied as inhibitory templates to shape ongoing processing.

A Unified Theory and Architecture for Systematic Generalization
This direction aims to overcome the fundamental limitations of systematic generalization in modern AI, such as the "reversal curse", by building integrated computational models of the cortical-hippocampal system. We will pursue a dual-path strategy. We will engineer biologically-plausible architectures grounded in Complementary Learning Systems theory, where fast-learning hippocampal modules interact with structured neocortical systems to transform specific experiences into abstract knowledge. Concurrently, we will formulate a "Cognitive Renormalization Group" theory to provide a unified mathematical framework that explains generalization as a dynamical phase transition emerging from multi-scale interactions within these circuits. Within this framework, the hippocampal indexing of discrete experiences corresponds to defining the system's "microstate", while the neocortical offline consolidation process implements a series of coarse-graining transformations. These transformations iteratively extract invariant features through a process that discards irrelevant details while preserving behaviorally relevant information, ultimately yielding stable, generalizable cognitive structures. The core of this direction is to reveal the fundamental computational principles, specifically the dynamics and phase transitions, that underlie the brain's ability to construct abstract, reusable knowledge from sparse data, thereby offering a blueprint for building artificial intelligence with human-like efficiency and robustness.

From Periodic Population Codes to Structured Symbols: A Dynamical Systems Theory for Neuro-Symbolic Computation
This direction aims to establish a framework that connects sub-symbolic neural dynamics and symbolic computation. We hypothesize that the nonlinear integration of multi-scale periodic population codes, such as the activity of grid cells with differing spatial frequencies, into stable fixed-point attractors constitutes a computational basis for compositional representation. The core objective is to formalize this dynamical transition, whereby continuous, periodic activity patterns are transformed into discrete, combinatorial symbols. We will develop a mathematical framework based on dynamical systems and group theory to characterize how phase coupling and competition between independent neural modules enable this binding process. The ultimate aim is to provide a foundational, biologically-grounded model for neuro-symbolic architectures, explaining how the core properties of symbols naturally emerge from the dynamics of neural populations.

Emergence and Control of Structured Representations in Neural Network
This direction aims to investigate how and why intelligent agents, such as neural networks, spontaneously develop internal representations that resemble symbols, variables, or data structures through end-to-end training without explicit symbolic supervision. The central goal is to characterize the conditions that favor the emergence of such structured representations, and to understand their fundamental properties. Building on this understanding, I will develop targeted mechanistic interventions not only to identify these representations but also to explore their potential for direct manipulation. The ultimate objective is to understand the mechanisms that allow neural networks to develop symbolic-like capabilities from data, paving the way for a new generation of AI systems that are both data-driven and symbolically grounded.

From Sensory Data to Causal Concepts: A Computational Model Based on Active Inference and Program Induction
This research aims to develop a computational model of causal learning that directly addresses the core question of how intelligent agents can form abstract, generalizable causal concepts from limited, concrete interactions. The model integrates active inference with probabilistic program induction within a Bayesian generative model framework. This framework enables the model to autonomously design and execute interventions in simulated environments to acquire causal evidence. Through probabilistic program induction, the model abstracts structured causal schemas from these specific experiences, a process that determines the meaning of a causal schema by its computational function during internal simulation, thereby providing a mechanistic account of how causal concepts emerge from interaction.

Inverse Inference of Mental Models from Language and Action
This direction aims to formalize a core inverse problem in social cognition: How can we infer an agent's latent beliefs and mental states from their observable linguistic explanations and actions? We propose to address this by framing it as a problem of Bayesian inverse inference over generative models. The core idea is that an agent's observable behavior and language are generated by an internal probabilistic generative model in which their goals and beliefs serve as priors and latent variables that give rise to observable actions. This inverse problem is solved by formulating "theory of mind" as a probabilistic generative model and inverting it through computational frameworks such as Bayesian inverse planning, thereby uncovering the latent mental structures that best explain the observations. The ultimate goal to advance a formal, mechanistic understanding of human cognition as approximate Bayesian inference, and to translate these principles into artificial intelligence that can reliably infer human intentions.
